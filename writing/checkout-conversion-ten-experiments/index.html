Checkout conversion: the ten experiments behind +11%

Inigo AnguloNovember 4, 20266 min read

The team was optimising the wrong step. Payment was instrumented to the pixel, and payment was fine. What nobody had measured was the gap: 38% of the people who eventually converted had left checkout at least once and come back, and when they came back the product treated them like strangers. No cart memory, no "you were here", a fresh start at step one.

That number came out of session recordings and a funnel rebuilt by user rather than by session. It changed the question from "how do we make payment faster" to "how do we make returning cheap". Ten experiments later, across four EU markets, checkout conversion was up 11%.

How the programme was run

Every experiment had a one page hypothesis before anything was built: what we believed, what would change, the metric, the sample size, the date we would read it. No peeking. No calling winners at 80% confidence because the quarter was ending. The losers were as useful as the winners, because each one closed a door the team had been arguing about for a year.

What moved the number

  • Re-entry. A returning visitor lands on the step they left, with their choices intact and one line acknowledging it. The single largest lift.
  • Trust at the moment of commitment. Cancellation terms and what happens after booking, shown where the decision is made, not in a tab.
  • Fewer fields, asked later. Everything that could be asked after payment was moved after payment.
  • Price without surprises. The total the user saw on the product page is the total at the end. Any change is explained before the button, not on the receipt.

What did not

  • A redesigned payment method selector. Flat. Payment was never the problem.
  • Urgency messaging on the last step. Slight negative on returns, and it felt wrong; killed.
  • A progress bar. Flat. People knew where they were; they did not know where their cart was.

The lesson I reuse on every funnel

Instrument the gaps, not the stages. Most analytics setups count people at each step and lose them between steps: the back button, the tab switch, the return visit three hours later. The leak is almost always there, and it is invisible until you rebuild the funnel per user. I have found the same shape since in a co-living booking flow (+34% after the redesign) and in a B2B onboarding. Different products, same blind spot.
