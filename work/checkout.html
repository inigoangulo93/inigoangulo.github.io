01 Context

The checkout wasn't broken where everyone thought

GetYourGuide processes millions of travel bookings per year. The checkout funnel was the company's highest-leverage surface: small improvements translate directly to significant revenue.

When I joined, the team had spent 6 months optimizing the payment form: button colors, field order, trust badges, copy variations. Results were flat. Single-digit basis point improvements that barely cleared significance.

The assumption was: the payment step is where we lose people. Everyone believed it. The dashboards seemed to confirm it. But nobody had looked at the full behavioral picture.

02 Insight

What the data actually showed

Week 1 notesThe checkout drop-off isn't a payment problem. It's a re-entry problem. PostHog session recordings show something nobody's measuring: 38% of converting users leave checkout at least once before buying. They compare prices, check calendars, text friends, come back days later. The funnel isn't linear. It's a loop. Hypothesis: fixing the re-entry experience will drive more uplift than any further payment optimization.

I set up a PostHog + GA4 experimentation program to track non-linear checkout behavior: something the existing analytics wasn't capturing because it assumed linear funnel progression.

Re-entry recovery rate: 31% to 52%. This single intervention drove roughly 7 of the 11 points of total uplift.

The discovery wasn't tactical. It was architectural: if your mental model of user behavior is wrong, every optimization built on it is wasted effort.

04 Solution

What I shipped

Persistent cart with 72h expiry + price-lock guarantee. Users returning saw their selection preserved with a clear "price locked" indicator. Addressed the #1 re-entry anxiety.

"Welcome back" state. Returning users saw what changed since their last visit (nothing / price change / availability update). Rebuilt confidence instantly.

Non-linear funnel tracking (PostHog + GA4). Instrumented checkout to measure loops, not linear progression. This framework became the standard after I left.

06 What failed

Three experiments that went wrong

Urgency timers killed conversionAdded countdown timers. Conversion dropped 4%. Users felt manipulated. Killed after 5 days. Urgency tactics from e-commerce don't work for 200+ euro experiences.
Personalized recs at checkout backfiredFirst "welcome back" showed recommendations alongside saved cart. Users clicked recs instead of completing. Simplified to cart-only. Completion for returning users jumped 18%. At checkout: reduce choices, don't add them.
Mobile is not small desktopFirst redesign was responsive desktop. PostHog showed mobile users re-enter via email/push, not URLs. Redesigned for deep-link entry. Mobile re-entry rate doubled.

What I'd do differently: Track cross-device re-entry from day one. Users start mobile, return desktop. We caught this late. Cost a month of learnings.

08 Raw thinking

Process notes

PostHog analysisSession recording sample: 200 converting users, 200 abandoners. Converting: 62% one session, 38% left and returned 1-3 times. Average return time: 1.8 days. Return trigger: direct URL (desktop), email/push (mobile). Abandoners: 44% never past activity selection. 31% reached checkout, bounced at cart review (NOT payment). 25% reached payment, failed (declined cards, wrong details). The 31% at cart review: this is the gold. Intent existed. Confidence broke. Not a "payment UX" problem. A "trust in persistence" problem.
CRO frameworkBuilt a framework the team still uses: 1. Map actual behavior (not assumed funnel) 2. Identify highest-friction transition 3. Categorize: cognitive / emotional / structural / technical 4. Design intervention for specific friction type 5. Measure with behavioral validation This caught the re-entry problem because step 1 revealed the funnel was non-linear.
