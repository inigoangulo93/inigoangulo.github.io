01 The starting point

A checkout everyone was fixing in the wrong place.

GetYourGuide processes millions of bookings a year across Europe. The checkout, from add to cart through payment confirmation, was the highest leverage surface in the product. Acquisition was strong. Conversion was not.

I joined the funnel as its only designer, embedded in the product team with the Lead PM and the Head of Development. The team was focused on the payment form. It was the obvious suspect: the last step, the one with the card fields. The funnel said otherwise.

02 What the recordings showed

People were not stuck. They were unsure.

So I stopped looking at the form and watched sessions instead. The same scene kept repeating. Someone reaches the total and pauses. They scroll back to the product page to check the price, come back, and on mobile the reload has wiped the summary and their progress. Two or three round trips later, they leave.

The doubt loop, from session recordings

ExitMost loops end off-platform: 38% of eventual converters left checkout at least once mid-loop, and nothing existed to bring the others back.
FixShow the math at the total (EXP-03) and keep the order summary persistent across steps (EXP-02). The loop dies when there is nothing left to verify.

That changed the question. Users were not dropping because of friction. They were dropping because of doubt, and three things were feeding it:

  • The price felt unverifiable. "All taxes included" was not enough. People wanted to see the math, so they went looking for it on the product page.
  • Mobile transitions broke the thread. Each step was a separate page load that lost the order, the timer and the progress. 65% of traffic was mobile.
  • Payment defaults ignored local habits. Credit card showed first everywhere. In the Netherlands 72% of online payments use iDEAL, in Poland BLIK dominates. Users scrolled past a whole form they did not want.
Evidence Hotjar sessions showed the anxiety loop: price → scroll up → price → leave. PostHog funnels revealed 8–12% drop-off at every mobile step transition. Payment method analysis showed large mismatches between defaults and local preferences.
03 The plan

Three causes became three bets. Ten experiments, and room to lose.

Each cause turned into a bet, and each bet into a short series of A/B tests. Nothing went in on taste. Every experiment had to trace back to something seen in sessions or funnels, with guardrail metrics tracked throughout.

06 The hard calls

Four calls where the cleaner idea had to prove itself.

Looking back across the log, four decisions carried most of the weight. Each one meant choosing between speed to ship and depth of change.

DecisionChosenRejectedWhy
Mobile architecture Two named steps with persistent context (EXP-02) Single-page checkout (EXP-08, killed); responsive tweaks to the 3-step flow 8–12% drop-off at every transition, so tweaks were not enough. The single-page version (EXP-08) added ~1.2s of render on mid-range Android and lost 0.9% on mobile. The principle, unbroken context, shipped as two named steps (EXP-02).
Price transparency Full itemized breakdown Better "taxes included" messaging Users wanted to see the math, not be told it was fair. Messaging didn't stop the anxiety loop.
Payment localization Per-market method order on a shared default (EXP-10) One global order; manual market-specific pages One system, not four maintained pages. It won in 2 of 4 markets, so it rolled out only where it won.
Urgency signals Honest hold-your-spot timer, the real hold time (EXP-01) Price-expiry countdown (EXP-06, killed) Same mechanic, opposite framing. The countdown won the click but degraded the trust guardrail and raised refund intent.
07 What happened

Small friction wasn't the problem. Lack of clarity was.

+11%Relative conversion uplift
10A/B tests run, 6 shipped
72%Of uplift came from mobile
Validation All experiments validated through A/B testing with statistical significance thresholds. Sample sizes ranged from 120K–140K users per variant. Guardrail metrics tracked throughout. The largest single win in the log was the hold-your-spot timer (EXP-01, +3.1% payment completion); the single-page checkout (EXP-08) was killed.
08 What I took from it

Once users understood what they were paying and where they were in the process, conversion followed.

The pattern Every winning experiment reduced uncertainty at the moment of commitment. Price breakdown, unbroken context, the right payment method, an honest hold timer. All of these removed cognitive friction at the exact moment the user was deciding whether to proceed. The framework: find the moment of highest commitment anxiety, then eliminate the uncertainty surrounding it.
What failed Post-booking cross-sell on the confirmation screen. Users who engaged had 3x higher LTV, but the timing was wrong. They'd just completed a cognitively expensive flow. The confirmation screen is a moment of relief, not decision-making. Lesson: respect the emotional state of the user at each point in the funnel.
