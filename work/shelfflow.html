01 The Problem

Launches took weeks. Writing was never the slow part.

One product launch at an enterprise CPG company means 6 SKUs × 3 retailers × 2 variants = 36 content packages, each with its own character limits, spec sheet and compliance rules. Doing this by hand took weeks. Versions lived in file names, and approvals moved through email threads nobody could reconstruct.

From the interviews In stakeholder interviews, roughly 70% of the launch delays people described started in approval ambiguity, not in content creation. That is what people reported, not an instrumented measurement. The question they kept asking was "who needs to sign off on this?"

One brief becomes 36 packages. Each product and retailer gets two variants, so someone has to choose which one ships: 18 choices before a single launch.

02 The Insight

A faster writer would not have helped. The team needed to know who decides.

Generative AI was already in the building, and it went unused for anything that mattered. The drafts were not the issue. Nobody had written down what the AI could decide on its own, what needed a person, and which person.

So I designed the decisions first and put the AI inside them. Six agents do the work: they parse, draft, check and flag. Four gates stand between them and the retailer, and each gate belongs to one named person. Maya Okafor, Content Lead, signs the brief and the drafts. Daniel Reyes, Compliance Officer, signs the claims. Sofia Lindqvist, Brand Manager, signs the launch. Alex Marín, the operator, runs the workflow and signs nothing. No agent can approve or publish.

The arrangement in one map: six agents above the line, the four gates and their owners below it.

03 The Product Decision

One canvas. The workflow is built on it, and then it runs there.

The obvious interface was a progress view: stages on a line and a bar that fills. It would have hidden what the team most needed to see, which is how the work is wired and where each person stands in it. So the canvas does two jobs. The operator composes the pipeline on it, and the same drawing then executes.

In Build mode the operator works from a library: agents, human gates, a split per retailer, a merge, a loop for work that is sent back, and a condition that routes regulated claims to an extra legal gate. The rules live in the canvas instead of a policy document. A gate needs a named owner before the launch can run, the four core gates cannot be deleted, and an agent cannot be wired to Publish.

The drawing also changes with the brief. A copy refresh on two retailers needs four agents. A seasonal promo needs new visuals, so the operator brings in an optional Image Generator that runs beside the Copy Drafter and arrives at the same gate. A new product line needs every check. A shape that worked should not be rebuilt by hand. Once a launch is live, the operator can save it as a template, and Brief Parser does the sorting: it compares past launches and splits every setting into what stays fixed, what comes filled in, and what is asked each time. The next promo is four fields instead of a brief.

Templates follow the rule the rest of the product follows: agents propose and a named person decides. The operator builds a template and the Content Lead publishes it. When launches drift away from it, or a retailer changes a limit, Diff Reporter proposes a new version with its evidence. Launches already running stay on the version they started with.

Alex submits the brief. The Brief Parser structures it into 6 SKUs and 36 packages, and stops on what it cannot settle: the brief names three markets, but every retailer and SKU is US only. Maya answers the question and locks the brief. From here it cannot change silently.

Gate 1. On the left, what Alex wrote. On the right, what the agent read, with every difference marked.

13:17 to 13:52 / Gate 2, drafts and a loop

The Copy Drafter writes two versions of all 36 packages. Maya sends three back as too long for Sephora. She says what is wrong in one sentence, the work returns to the agent, and she is free until it comes back. Minutes later the rewrites return with the changes marked, and she approves all 36.

A rejection is a loop, not a dead end. Three drafts are back with the Copy Drafter and everything downstream waits.

14:10 to 14:21 / The checks

Past Gate 2 the work splits by retailer. Three Spec Checkers run in parallel against each retailer's limits, and the merge waits for all of them. Then the Compliance Scanner reads every claim. Agents stay quiet on the canvas: a small node, a name and one fact. The detail is one click away.

The split while it runs. Amazon is done, Sephora and Walmart are still being checked.

14:21 to 14:52 / Gate 3, compliance

The scanner raises four flags: "clinically proven", "results in 14 days", "dermatologist approved" and "all-day protection". The other 32 packages are clear. The flags are what the agent caught, not what shipped. Daniel takes them one at a time.

Gate 3 opens where it sits on the graph and says whose turn it is.

No regulated claim is left, so the legal branch is skipped. Sofia now chooses which of the two variants ships for each product and retailer: 18 choices, taken one at a time. A shortcut can apply her choice to the rest, and it stays locked until she has opened each one.

05 What I Got Wrong

Two things v1 got wrong, and what testing showed.

The launch above is the second version. Both mistakes in the first one had the same root: I treated control as something you add, when it is something people have to be able to carry.

Six gates Sawv1 had 6 decision gates, one per stage. In testing, content leads approved two of them, at brief intake and after retailer adaptation, within seconds. They were signing, not deciding.ChangedI cut to 4 gates, placed where a real decision happens. What the two removed gates looked at is still checked, by agents, and reaches a person at the next gate.NowA gate only exists where someone can say no for a reason. On the canvas you can add one, and the first thing the tool asks is who signs it.
Approving in bulk Sawv1 let reviewers approve packages in bulk without opening them. In testing they did exactly that to save time.ChangedThe shortcut stays locked until each remaining package has been opened, and a review-time floor per cell marks approvals that come too fast.NowYou can test it in the demo in section 03: press the shortcut early and it refuses, and the refusal is written to the audit trail.

The test sessions are under NDA, so this page shows the mechanism each finding produced, not the recordings.

How we knew How we measured the agents. Agent quality was tracked against human outcomes, not benchmarks: flag precision (how often a compliance flag survived human review), per-gate rejection rate as a drift signal, regeneration acceptance (how often a v2 shipped without further notes), and a review-time floor per cell, the rubber-stamp detector that triggered the batch guardrail. When rejection rates fell too low, we treated it as reviewer fatigue, not agent excellence.
06 What It Demonstrates

The work did not get smaller. Every step of it got an owner.

Before, a launch moved through file names and email threads, and nobody could say who had approved what. In the demo launch the brief arrives at 13:02 and Sofia signs at 15:24. Six agents produce and check 36 packages in between, and three people make the decisions instead of reading 36 documents: Maya settles one question and three rewrites, Daniel resolves four flags, Sofia makes 18 choices.

Those figures come from the demo launch; the client's are under NDA. What the case can show is the mechanism. The AI did not earn trust here by writing better. It earned it once the team could see what it was allowed to decide, and who answered for everything else.
