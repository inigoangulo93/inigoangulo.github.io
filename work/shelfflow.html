Enterprise Commerce / AI Workflow Systems / Decision Architecture

The operating system for commerce content at scale. One brief in, retailer-ready content out, with humans owning every decision that ships.

ShelfFlow is a structured workflow platform for enterprise commerce teams. It coordinates AI-assisted content generation across SKUs, retailers, and markets, while keeping human authority over every approval that reaches the shelf. I led product design end-to-end: workflow architecture, state machine design, decision authority mapping, AI orchestration model, and the full operator interface.

01 Problem

Enterprise commerce teams were producing content manually, SKU by SKU, retailer by retailer. It didn't scale.

A single product launch at an enterprise CPG company generates content across multiple SKUs, multiple retailers, and multiple markets. Each retailer has different spec requirements, character limits, image guidelines, and compliance rules. The content team was doing this by hand.

One brief would take weeks to turn into retailer-ready packages. Errors compounded. Versioning was manual. Approvals happened over email threads no one could follow. The bottleneck wasn't creativity — it was coordination.

  • 6 SKUs × 6 retailers = 36 content packages from a single product brief. Each with unique spec requirements.
  • No single source of truth. Content lived in spreadsheets, email threads, and shared drives. Version conflicts were constant.
  • Approval chains were invisible. No one knew who had approved what, or what was still pending. Launches slipped.
  • AI tools existed but weren't trusted. Teams experimented with generative AI but had no governance model. Nothing AI-generated shipped without manual rewriting.
02 Insight

The problem wasn't content generation. It was decision architecture. Who approves what, when, and with what authority.

Every enterprise content workflow has the same hidden structure: a series of decision gates where someone must say yes or no before work moves forward. The teams I observed didn't lack tools — they lacked a system that made decision authority explicit.

AI could accelerate the generation phase, but only if humans retained clear authority over what ships. The insight: design the decision architecture first, then embed AI as an accelerator within it.

Evidence Stakeholder interviews revealed that 70% of delays weren't in content creation — they were in approval ambiguity. "Who needs to sign off on this?" was the most common question. The workflow needed explicit gates, not faster drafting.
03 System

One brief in, 36 retailer-ready packages out. Six stages, four decision gates, zero ambiguity.

ShelfFlow is a structured workflow platform. It takes a single product brief and coordinates the entire content production pipeline across SKUs, retailers, and markets. The system has 6 workflow stages with 4 mandatory decision gates where human approval is required before content advances.

Six-stage pipeline

S1Brief IntakeProduct brief parsed, SKU matrix generated
S2AI Draft Generation◆ Gate 1: Brief approval
S3Human Review & Edit◆ Gate 2: Draft quality
S4Retailer AdaptationSpecs, character limits, compliance
S5Compliance & Legal◆ Gate 3: Compliance sign-off
S6Publish & Distribute◆ Gate 4: Final launch approval

End-to-end workflow architecture. 6 stages, 4 decision gates, AI intelligence layer embedded throughout.

04 Workflow

The operator interface: one screen to track 36 packages across 6 stages.

The core interface is a workflow board where operators see every content package, its current stage, who owns it, and what's blocking it. Each package card shows its SKU, target retailer, current gate status, and AI confidence score.

The design principle: at any moment, an operator should be able to answer "what's blocking this launch?" in under 5 seconds. No drilling into sub-screens. No checking email. The board is the single source of truth.

  • Package cards show SKU, retailer, stage, owner, gate status, and time-in-stage. Color-coded by urgency.
  • Batch operations let operators approve, reject, or reassign multiple packages at once. Critical when managing 36 packages per launch.
  • Filters by retailer, SKU, or gate status so operators can focus on what needs attention now.

05 States

Every content package has a state machine. No package moves without an explicit transition.

Each of the 36 content packages operates as an independent state machine. States are explicit, transitions are guarded by gate conditions, and every state change is logged with who triggered it and why.

Content package states

DraftAI-generatedAwaiting review
In ReviewHuman editingOwner assigned
ApprovedGate passedReady for next stage
PublishedRetailer-liveAudit trail complete
RejectedGate failedReturns to previous stage with notes
BlockedDependency unmetWaiting on external input
Design rationale Every state transition is logged: who, when, why. Rejected packages carry reviewer notes back to the previous stage. No silent failures. No packages stuck in limbo. The state machine is the accountability layer.

SKU lifecycle: 8 possible states, 2 loops (fix + re-scan), per-retailer approval. 36 parallel state machines converging to a single launch.

06 Decisions

What trade-offs did I make?

Every decision involved choosing between speed and control. In enterprise commerce, the cost of shipping wrong content to a retailer is higher than the cost of one more approval step.

DecisionChosenRejectedWhy
AI authority Draft-only (never ships) Auto-publish with confidence threshold Enterprise compliance requires human sign-off. AI that ships autonomously is a liability, not a feature.
Gate model 4 mandatory gates, no bypass Flexible approval chains Flexibility introduces ambiguity. "Who approved this?" must always have one answer.
Package granularity 1 package = 1 SKU × 1 retailer Grouped by SKU or by retailer Retailer specs diverge too much. Grouping hides critical differences. Granular = traceable.
State machine Explicit states, logged transitions Implicit progress tracking When a retailer asks "who approved this copy?", the system must answer in seconds, not hours.

Decision authority map: who owns which decisions across the workflow. AI agents act autonomously between gates — humans own every approval that ships.

07 AI Layer

Six AI agents. Each has a defined scope. None can ship content.

ShelfFlow embeds 6 specialised AI agents across the pipeline. Each agent operates within a bounded scope: it can draft, suggest, check, or flag — but it cannot approve or publish. Human authority is not a fallback. It's the architecture.

Embedded AI agents

BRIEF PARSERExtracts SKU matrix, claims, and key messages from product brief
COPY DRAFTERGenerates retailer-adapted copy per SKU. Respects character limits and tone.
SPEC CHECKERValidates against retailer spec sheets. Flags violations before human review.
COMPLIANCE SCANNERChecks claims against regulatory databases. Flags risk, never clears it.
CONSISTENCY AUDITORCross-checks messaging across all 36 packages. Detects contradictions.
DIFF REPORTERSummarises what changed between versions. Enables faster re-approval.

Every agent drafts or flags. No agent approves. Zero autonomous shipping.

AI agent orchestration: how intelligence flows through the content pipeline. Workflow orchestrator routes, schedules, and enforces gates.

Human-in-the-loop decision model. Three zones: AI acts freely (parse, scan, format), AI proposes (enrichments, variants), human decides (validate, approve, ship). Trust architecture below.

08 Complexity

36 packages. 18 approvals. The combinatorial explosion that makes manual workflows collapse.

The core complexity of ShelfFlow isn't any single content package — it's the combinatorial explosion when you multiply SKUs by retailers by markets. A "simple" 6-SKU launch across 6 retailers generates 36 unique content packages, each needing at least 3 human touch points.

This is where manual workflows break. Not at package #1, but at package #27, when reviewer fatigue sets in and the differences between Amazon and Walmart specs blur together. ShelfFlow's job is to make package #36 as reviewable as package #1.

Scale math 6 SKUs × 6 retailers = 36 packages. Each package passes through 4 gates. That's 144 gate evaluations per launch. With ~50% requiring at least one revision cycle, the system manages 200+ state transitions per product launch. Without structured workflow, this is untrackable.

The content matrix explosion: 1 brief → 12 SKUs × 3 retailers × 2 variants = 36 content packages → 18 approval decisions → 1 launch.

09 Ecosystem

ShelfFlow sits between the brand team and the retailer. It's the coordination layer, not a content tool.

The platform integrates with the existing enterprise ecosystem: PIM systems for product data, DAM systems for assets, retailer portals for final distribution, and compliance databases for claim validation. ShelfFlow doesn't replace these systems — it orchestrates the workflow between them.

  • Upstream: Product briefs, PIM data, brand guidelines, regulatory databases
  • Core: ShelfFlow workflow engine, AI agents, decision gates, state machine
  • Downstream: Retailer portals (Amazon, Walmart, Target, etc.), content delivery, audit logs

ShelfFlow in the commerce content ecosystem. Upstream sources (PIM, DAM, brand guidelines) flow in; retailer-ready content flows out to Amazon, Sephora, Walmart, and any retail channel.

10 Service Design

Four roles, clear authority boundaries. No one can do someone else's job.

ShelfFlow defines four operator roles, each with explicit permissions mapped to workflow stages and gate authority. Role boundaries are enforced by the system, not by process documentation.

Authority mapping

CONTENT LEADOwns briefs, approves drafts (G1, G2)
COMPLIANCEOwns regulatory review (G3)
BRAND MANAGERFinal launch authority (G4)
OPERATORManages workflow, no gate authority

Service blueprint: 5 operational layers (user actions, frontstage UI, backstage AI, system infra, human ops) mapped across all 6 workflow stages.

11 Design Principles

The rules that shaped every design decision in ShelfFlow.

  • Humans approve, AI accelerates. No AI agent can advance content past a decision gate. The human is not a fallback — they are the system.
  • Every state transition is auditable. If a retailer asks "who approved this claim?", the system answers in seconds.
  • The board is the source of truth. If it's not on the board, it doesn't exist. No side channels, no email approvals.
  • Package #36 must be as reviewable as package #1. Design against reviewer fatigue, not reviewer enthusiasm.
  • Complexity is the system's problem, not the operator's. 36 packages, 144 gate evaluations — the operator sees what needs attention now.
12 Reflection

The hardest design problem wasn't the AI. It was making 18 approval moments feel fast instead of bureaucratic.

The pattern AI in enterprise workflows fails when it's positioned as a replacement for human judgement. It succeeds when it's positioned as an accelerator within an explicit decision architecture. The value of ShelfFlow isn't that AI writes copy — it's that the system makes the coordination overhead of 36 packages manageable. The workflow is the product.
What failed v1 had 6 decision gates — one per stage. Testing revealed that gates at Brief Intake and Retailer Adaptation added friction without adding value. Content leads were rubber-stamping them. Reduced to 4 gates where genuine decision-making happens. Also: the first version of batch operations let users approve packages they hadn't individually reviewed. Added a "reviewed" flag requirement before batch approval. Speed without review is liability.
