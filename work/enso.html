Act one

The operator's question: can I trust this person?

Phase 1. A broken onboarding, rebuilt around the three decisions operators were really making.

01 The brief

The brief said: move 47 spreadsheets to digital.

ENSO Co-Living operates in Lisbon, Valencia, Madrid and Barcelona, matching tenants to shared apartments by lifestyle, language, budget and timing. When I joined, onboarding ran on 47 spreadsheets and 3 different workflows across four cities. Everyone assumed the workflow was correct and only needed to move to digital.

02 What shadowing showed

Operators were making 3 decisions per applicant, not 47.

Two weeks of operator shadowing proved the brief wrong. Operators were not following the spreadsheets. They had built informal shortcuts. The spreadsheets documented an ideal process. The operators had evolved a real one.

Underneath it were three questions: is this person real, do they fit an available room, and would they be a good roommate? Everything else was administrative noise accumulated over two years of patching. The bottleneck was trust, not data.

"I ignore half the spreadsheet. I just need to know: can I picture this person living with the current tenants?" Valencia operations manager, during shadowing
The real process
Three decisions, one per trust question
01Is this person real?Registration and sign in
02Do they fit a room?City, dates and living style
03Would they be a good roommate?A 1 minute video intro
Result: 48 hours became under 5 minutes, 6 steps became 3, and four cities run on one flow.
Trust map: who needs to trust whom before a room fills
Operator, per city Phase 1 Is this person real, do they fit a room, will they be a good roommate? The gatekeeper. Owns the relationship with every flat. Was answering all three questions off the record, in WhatsApp threads and informal calls.
Current housemates Represented by the operator Who is moving in with us? Never in the funnel, always in the decision. The operator’s real test was whether they could picture the applicant living with them.
Applicant Phase 2 Is this room worth committing to, and is what I’m seeing true? Booking a home in another city, often from abroad and on mobile. Any signal that looks manufactured breaks the deal.
ENSO Phase 2 How fast does an empty room fill? Every empty room costs about €4.2K a month. The business clock runs underneath every other relationship.

Phase 1 answered the operator’s questions. Phase 2 answered the applicant’s, under the business clock. Both phases are the same service seen from two sides.

03 The service, redrawn

Same five stages. Two completely different services.

Before designing a single screen, I mapped the shadowing into a service blueprint: what the applicant does, what they see, what the operator does behind the line of visibility, and which systems hold it together. Drawn this way, the problem stopped being “too many spreadsheets”. The trust signal operators relied on lived entirely off the record, in WhatsApp threads and ad hoc calls, so it could not be measured, shared or scaled.

The redesign kept the stages and moved the trust signal into the product. Toggle between both versions.

05 The operator side

The applicant flow takes five minutes. The operator flow is where trust is decided.

Most onboarding work stops at the applicant’s screens. Here the critical journey was backstage: one operator per city reviewing every applicant on their own schedule. I designed the review as a single view that answers the three trust questions in order, with an explicit outcome for every answer, so no applicant ends in silence.

Operator review: one view, three questions, four outcomes
1
Is this person real?Registration data and sign-in arrive verified and structured. Nothing to re-key, nothing to chase.
2
Do they fit an available room?City, dates and living style sit next to the inventory they could match. The operator judges fit, not data quality.
3
Would they be a good roommate?The 1-minute video replaces the informal call. Same signal operators already trusted, now async and attached to the profile.
ApprovePersonalised catalogue for their city, ready to book.Happy path
Need more signalAsk for a new video or a short call, then back to review.Recovery path
No room fits yetProfile stays qualified and resurfaces when a matching room opens.Feeds the catalogue loop
Not a fitA clear, respectful close. No ghosting.Closure path
Why the operator stays in the loop I also explored AI powered roommate matching to automate step 3. Operators rejected it: it took away the judgment their relationship with each flat depends on. The final design uses the product to prepare the decision and leaves the decision with the person accountable for it.

Act two

The applicant's question: can I trust this room?

Phase 2. With trusted demand flowing in, the catalogue became the bottleneck, and urgency had to be earned.

07 The next bottleneck

Every empty room is burning money. Static listings weren't helping.

With onboarding solved, the next bottleneck was clear: the catalogue. Every habi costs ENSO ~4.2K€/month when empty. Our listing page treated every room equally. A high-risk vacancy that needed to fill this week looked identical to one available three months out.

No urgency differentiation. No conversion pressure calibrated to actual business risk. In Q3 alone, 23% of rooms went past their target move-in date.

~4.2K€lost per month for every room that sits empty
23%of rooms went past their target move-in date in Q3
HypothesisIf we surface the right urgency signals at the right time, we can accelerate bookings for high-risk inventory without degrading trust.
The bridge

Onboarding and the catalogue are two ends of one loop.

Seen as screens, Phase 1 and Phase 2 look like separate projects. Seen as a service, they are the same loop. Every room moves through a cycle from the day it gets a target move-in date to the day someone moves in. Phase 1 built a steady supply of trusted, qualified applicants. Phase 2 pointed that supply at the rooms costing the most.

08 The risk system

Five risk tiers. Progressive urgency, calibrated to real risk.

Five risk tiers drive the progressive addition of urgency elements. Each tier maps to days remaining before vacancy becomes a financial liability. Levels 1 to 4 are additive: each inherits the elements of the level below. Level 5 does not add pressure: it swaps the heaviest perks for a flexible entry date.

Three principles
What the card is allowed to do
Progressive disclosureStart clean and minimal. Urgency elements are added as business risk increases.
Truthful signals onlyReal dates, real deals, real perks. Nothing fabricated.
Scarcity, not pressureContext to decide with confidence, never manufactured panic.

Guardrails

Urgency only counts if it doesn’t cost trust later.

A conversion lift that turns into cancellations, tickets or regret is just a delayed cost. So the service had to be measured past the booking, at the moments where manufactured urgency usually shows up.

Early cancellation rate4.8%4.6%No increase in people backing out after booking.
Support tickets per week1211Signals were clear enough not to create questions.
Post-booking NPS6265Slight improvement. No booking regret.
Average discount depth13.6%Within margin. Monitored per tier.

Control vs variant, same 28-day experiment.

The test that shaped the principles Early explorations used countdown timers. Four out of five testers said it felt like a hotel booking site pushing them. That is where “scarcity, not pressure” came from: show what is real, like real deals and real availability, and never invent time pressure.
10 Results and reflection

Once I framed the process as 3 trust decisions instead of 47 data fields, the right product became obvious.

+34%Booking conversion
ValidationPostHog A/B test. 14,208 users, 28 days, 95% significance on Day 16. All guardrails passed. No increase in cancellations, support load, or booking regret.
The pattern The brief is almost never the problem. "Digitize 47 spreadsheets" was a symptom described as a task. Scope discipline wasn't a constraint. It was the strategy. Shipping 5 things that matter beats shipping 13 things that cover the roadmap.
What failed AI powered roommate matching failed because operators felt it undermined their trust relationship with tenants. "Smart" features that remove human judgment from high-trust decisions create resistance, not efficiency. Also underestimated design system investment, and custom components couldn't be reused when supporting the expansion to London.
One signal I would rebuild The test measured the whole bundle, so I cannot say how much of the lift each signal carried. The one I would rebuild first is the viewer count: today I would use signals that stay true when nobody is watching, like calendar scarcity, saves this month or a deal with a real end date.
What I'd do differently Start the blueprint with the operator, not the applicant: the backstage is where the service was really broken. Design mobile only from day one, since 73% of catalogue traffic was mobile. Track cross-device journeys from launch. And test urgency signals one by one instead of as a bundle, to know which ones carry the lift.
