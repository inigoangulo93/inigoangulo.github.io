How a build runs

  1. Define and scopeOne week. What the product must do, what it must never do, the data model and the security boundaries. Written as tickets with acceptance criteria. If you are not sure what to build yet, the product opportunity assessment below is this step on its own.
  2. Build with gatesAgents work in isolated branches. Every change passes lint, types, tests, a secrets scan and a review before it reaches main. No agent has production credentials.
  3. Human approvalReleases are promoted by a person, after a security pass. The approval is one id and one letter, and it is logged.
  4. Ship and measureProduction from week two or three. Analytics and an audit log from day one, so we know what is used.
  5. Hand over or operateDocumentation, decision log and the agent playbook. Your team keeps the system, or I keep operating it for a period.
A first step that fits most situations

Product opportunity assessment. Two weeks to understand the business opportunity, identify what should be built, define an initial scope, name the risks and dependencies, and recommend an execution approach. It stands on its own, and a build that starts from it starts from a written scope instead of a conversation.

Questions I get asked

Is this no code?

No. It is real code in a real repository, with tests and a release process, written mostly by AI agents under my direction and review. You own it and any engineer can continue it.

How do you keep agents from breaking production?

Isolation and gates. Agents never hold production credentials, every change passes automated checks and a review, and promotion to production is a human decision with a security report attached. The Acervo case documents the whole flow.

What is included in a typical engagement?

Scope, design, build, testing, security review, deployment and a documented handover. The first version is a sprint of two to six weeks; after that, either your team continues or I keep shipping for a period. Agent costs are a few hundred francs a month and I pass them through at cost.

What does 17 days to production prove, and what does it not?

That one person with a scoped product and the right gates can have a thin first version in production in under three weeks. It does not mean a finished product: the fifteen releases after it are where most of the product lives.

Which stack?

TypeScript, Next.js, Postgres (Supabase), Vercel for most products, because agents are strongest there and the ecosystem is boring in the good way. I adapt to yours when there is a reason.

Do you work on site?

Hybrid near Zurich, remote across Europe. A build needs one kickoff in the room and weekly demos; the rest does not.
