See the system.
Design the decision.

Every product is a decision-making system wearing an interface as a mask. I take the mask off, redesign the system underneath, then put it back on. This is what that looks like.

01

Every interface is a mask over a system

Screens are what users see. Underneath is a web of data flows, business rules, edge cases, and human behavior patterns. I start at the network level, not the surface. The system architecture determines the interface. Never the other way around.

3 boxes on top, 15 nodes underneath. If you only design the boxes, you're decorating, not designing. See how this played out at ENSO
02

The best design is what you remove

Every feature, metric, and option fights for cognitive space. 47 spreadsheets became 3 decisions. 23 dashboard metrics became 1 status light. A 6-step checkout became a single re-entry moment. Clarity is not simplicity. Clarity is knowing what to kill.

The faded boxes are real features we considered. The glowing one shipped. That ratio is the job. KODO went from 23 metrics to 1
03

Watch what people do, ignore what they say

The smooth line is the story stakeholders tell. The erratic one is what actually happens. The ripples mark the moments where the gap is largest. That's where every real design insight is hiding. Session recordings over interviews. Behavioral data over opinions. Always.

Everyone said "payment is the problem." PostHog showed re-entry was. That gap was worth +11%. Read the checkout case study
04

Every wrong answer tightens the orbit

Failure is not noise. It's signal. Each wrong attempt eliminates a region of possibility and pulls closer to the answer. The red orbit is wide and expensive. The teal one is tight and precise. The core is the shipped product. Getting there fast means failing with intention.

I ship failure reports as design artifacts. They're worth more than the final mockup because they contain the reasoning the mockup can't show.
See the system + Subtract ruthlessly + Measure behavior + Fail fast = Systems that perform

This is how I've worked for 10 years. The domains change. The principle doesn't.
